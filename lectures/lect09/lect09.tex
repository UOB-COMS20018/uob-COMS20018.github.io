\begin{titledslide}{Agenda}

We're going to look at two papers which are important in the history
of the philosophy of AI:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \href{https://www.cs.ox.ac.uk/activities/ieg/e-library/sources/t_article.pdf}{\emph{Computing Machinery and Intelligence} by Alan Turing,
  \emph{Mind}, 59, 433-460, 1950}.
\item \href{https://web-archive.southampton.ac.uk/cogprints.org/7150/1/10.1.1.83.5248.pdf}{\emph{Minds, brains, and programs} by John Searle, \emph{Behavioral and
    Brain Sciences} 3 (3): 417-457, 1980}.
\end{enumerate}

The link above to Searle's paper only takes you to a pre-print which
does not contain critiques of Searle by other authors. To get the full
paper (as a PDF), you need to go via the University library.

\end{titledslide}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{titledslide}{Asking ``Can machines think?''}

  \begin{itemize}
  \item Turing starts his paper as follows:
  \end{itemize}

  \begin{quotation}
    I propose to consider the question, ``Can machines think?'' This
    should begin with definitions of the meaning of the terms
    ``machine'' and ``think.''
  \end{quotation}

  This is a typical approach in philosophy: first consider what we
  even mean by the question we are investigating.
  
\end{titledslide}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{titledslide}{Not asking ``Can machines think?''}

  \begin{itemize}
  \item Turing quickly decides that the question ``Can machines
    think?'' is too ambiguous.
  \item He later says it is: ``too meaningless to deserve discussion''
  \item The key innovation of his \emph{Mind} paper is to
    \textbf{replace} the original question with another ``which is
    closely related to it and is expressed in relatively unambiguous
    words.''
  \end{itemize}
  
\end{titledslide}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{titledslide}{The Imitation Game}

  \begin{itemize}
  \item Turing imagines a game where a machine attempts to trick an
    interrogator into thinking it to be human.
  \item Turing calls it ``The Imitation Game'', later it became known
    as the ``Turing Test''.
  \item Turing's new question is: what will happen when a machine
    plays the imitation game?
  \end{itemize}
  
\end{titledslide}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{titledslide}{What sort of machine?}

  \begin{itemize}
  \item ``we wish to exclude from the machines men born in the usual
    manner.''
  \item ``we only permit digital computers to take part in our game.''
  \item Turing then goes on to demonstrate the universality of Turing
    machines (although he doesn't call them that!)
  \end{itemize}
  
\end{titledslide}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{titledslide}{Turing's prediction on the Turing test}

  \begin{quote}
    I believe that in about fifty years' time it will be possible, to
    programme computers, with a storage capacity of about 109, to make
    them play the imitation game so well that an average interrogator
    will not have more than 70 per cent chance of making the right
    identification after five minutes of questioning
  \end{quote}

  \begin{itemize}
  \item OK, so had the `Turing test' been passed in $\sim$ 2000?
  \item Has the `Turing test' now been passed?
  \end{itemize}
  
\end{titledslide}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{titledslide}{Turing's prediction on ``educated opinion''}

  \begin{quote}
    \dots I believe that at the end of the century the use of words
    and general educated opinion will have altered so much that one
    will be able to speak of machines thinking without expecting to be
    contradicted.
  \end{quote}

  \begin{itemize}
  \item Was he right?
  \end{itemize}
  
\end{titledslide}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{titledslide}{Arguments against Turing machines thinking: Uncomputability}

  \begin{itemize}
  \item Turing machines are limited: there are uncomputable problems,
    e.g.\ the Halting Problem
  \item But what about human limitations?
  \end{itemize}
  
\end{titledslide}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{titledslide}{Arguments against Turing machines thinking:
    Discreteness}

  \begin{itemize}
  \item Computers are discrete, whereas brains are continuous.
  \item A discrete machine can approximate continuous quantities to
    any desired degree of precision (consider floating point numbers)
  \end{itemize}
  
\end{titledslide}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{titledslide}{Arguments against Turing machines thinking:
    Lovelace's objection}


  \begin{itemize}
  \item Ada Lovelace: machines just do what we tell them to do.
  \item In the section on ``Learning Machines'', Turing says:
  \end{itemize}

  \begin{quote}
    Instead of trying to produce a programme to simulate the adult
    mind, why not rather try to produce one which simulates the
    child's? If this were then subjected to an appropriate course of
    education one would obtain the adult brain.
  \end{quote}

  \begin{itemize}
  \item Turing postulates a machine learning rules (symbolic learning)
    using a form of reinforcement learning.
  \end{itemize}
\end{titledslide}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%% 
\begin{titledslide}{Machine learning of natural language}

  \begin{quote}
    It can also be maintained that it is best to provide the machine
    with the best sense organs that money can buy, and then teach it
    to understand and speak English. This process could follow the
    normal teaching of a child. Things would be pointed out and named,
    etc.
  \end{quote}

  \begin{itemize}
  \item How does this relate to how LLMs are trained?
  \item There's an argument here for \emph{embodied AI}.
  \end{itemize}
    
\end{titledslide}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{titledslide}{Searle's claim}


  \begin{enumerate}
  \item ``Intentionality in human beings (and animals) is a product of
    causal features of the brain.''
  \item ``Instantiating a computer program is never by itself a
    sufficient condition of intentionality''
  \end{enumerate}

  Also: ``\dots \emph{only} a machine could think, and only very
  special kinds of machines, namely brains and machines with internal
  causal powers equivalent to those of brains.'' and ``no program by
  itself is sufficient for thinking''.
  
\end{titledslide}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{titledslide}{The Chinese room}


  \begin{itemize}
  \item Searle imagines himself locked in a room with 3 batches of
    Chinese writing.
  \item Searle is completely ignorant of the Chinese language (spoken
    or written) and cannot even distinguish Chinese characters from
    ``meaningless squiggles''.
  \item Unknown to Searle, the 3 batches are a ``script'', a ``story''
    and some questions about the story.
  \item Searle is given instructions in English (``the program'') that
    enable him to ``correlate elements'' of what's in the 3 batches of
    Chinese writing to generate answers (in Chinese) to the questions.
    
  \end{itemize}
  
\end{titledslide}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{titledslide}{The Chinese room (continued)}

  Searle imagines that:
  \begin{quote}
    I get so good at following the instructions for manipulating the
    Chinese symbols and the programmers get so good at writing the
    programs that from the external point of view - that is, from the
    point of view of somebody outside the room in which I am locked -
    my answers to the questions are absolutely indistinguishable from
    those of native Chinese speakers. Nobody just looking at my
    answers can tell that I don't speak a word of Chinese
  \end{quote}

and continues:
  
  \begin{quote}
    As far as the Chinese is concerned, I simply behave like a
    computer; I perform computational operations on formally specified
    elements. For the purposes of the Chinese, I am simply an
    instantiation of the computer program.
  \end{quote}

\end{titledslide}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{titledslide}{Searle's main argument}

  \begin{quote}
    \dots whatever purely formal principles you put into the computer,
    they will not be sufficient for understanding, since a human will
    be able to follow the formal principles without understanding
    anything.
  \end{quote}
  
\end{titledslide}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{titledslide}{Abelson's critique}

  \begin{quote}
    the understanding is in the programmer's rule set, which the
    computer exercises
  \end{quote}

  \begin{quote}
    I would be willing to bet that by the Chinese symbol test, most
    of the people reading this don't really understand the
    transcendental number e, or economic inflation, or nuclear power
    plant safety, or how sailboats can sail upwind. (Be honest with
    yourself!)
  \end{quote}
  
  \begin{quote}
    At what point does a person graduate from ``merely'' manipulating
    rules to ``really'' understanding?
  \end{quote}
  
\end{titledslide}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{titledslide}{Block's critique}

  \begin{quote}
    \dots his argument does not even have the right \emph{form}, for
    in order to know whether we should reject the doctrine because of
    its alleged counterintuitive consequences, we must know what sort
    of evidence there is in \emph{favor} of the doctrine. If the
    evidence for the doctrine is overwhelming, then incompatible
    intuitions should be ignored.
  \end{quote}

  \begin{quote}
    Does Searle really think that it is an initially congenial idea
    that a hunk of gray jelly is the seat of his intentionality?
    (Could one imagine a less likely candidate?) What makes gray jelly
    so intuitively satisfying to Searle is obviously his knowledge
    that brains are the seat of \emph{our} intentionality. But here we see
    the difficulty in relying on considered intuitions, namely that
    they depend on our beliefs, and among the beliefs most likely to
    play a role in the case at hand are precisely our doctrines about
    whether the formal symbol-manipulation theory of thinking is true
    or false.
\end{quote}

\end{titledslide}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{titledslide}{Dennett's critique}

  Dennett argues that the valid part of Searle's argument is not new:
  
  \begin{quote}
    ``A computer whose only input and output was verbal would always
    be blind to the meaning of what was written'' (Dennett 1969,
    p. 182). The idea has been around for a long time.
\end{quote}

Also:

\begin{quote}
  Searle has confused different levels of explanation (and
  attribution). \emph{I} understand English; my brain doesn't \dots
\end{quote}

If we suppose ``the hero in the Chinese room'' is more like a robot:
\begin{quote}
  Are we to imagine that when he responds to the Chinese for ``pass
  the salt, please'' by getting his hand to grasp the salt and move it
  in a certain direction, he doesn't notice that this is what he is
  doing? In short, could anyone who became accomplished in this
  imagined exercise fail to become fluent in Chinese in the process?
\end{quote}
\end{titledslide}
